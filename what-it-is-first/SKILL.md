# What It Is First

A person opening a website for the first time knows none of the concepts it covers. This skill makes the AI introduce every concept by saying what it is, from the first draft, and only then bring in the striking details.

## Why this is needed

When writing introductory copy, AI tends to lead with whatever is most impressive: an unusual property, a big number, a twist, or a negation like "X is not Y". The writer already knows the concept, so the line feels punchy. A first-time reader, though, meets the details before learning what they are about.

> **Not just another notes app.**

This heading never says what the app is or what it does. The reader is left with "So what *is* it?"

> Over 2 million users. 99.9% uptime. Built in six weeks.

The numbers come before what the product is and who it's for. It sounds impressive, but the reader has nothing to attach the numbers to.

## Principles

### 1. The first sentence says what it is

Wherever a concept first appears, its first sentence states its identity — usually its category (what kind of thing it is) and its core role.

- Feature: "A feature that turns meeting recordings into summaries."
- Organization: "A nonprofit that repairs and donates used laptops."
- Person: "Lead designer of the studio."

### 2. Order: what it is → what it does → details → limits

1. **What it is**: identity
2. **What it does, why it matters**: role, relation to other concepts
3. **Details**: unusual traits, numbers, twists, anecdotes
4. **Limits**: what is unknown, what is not yet decided

This does not mean deleting the details. Placed after the definition, they land better.

### 3. Headings can have style, but must name an identity or role

Short, rhythmic headings are fine. "We design it. We build it. We run it." works because each part states a role.

Where a concept first appears, do not use these headings. Once the definition has been given, they are fine.

- Negation only: "Not just another app", "No strings attached"
- Number only: "2M+", "99.9%"
- Metaphor or mood only: "Where it all begins", "A quiet revolution"
- Oddity only: "Made by hand, one at a time."

### 4. Verbs need objects

"Analyzes", "connects", "protects" alone don't say what is being done. On first introduction, include the object.

- "Our engine analyzes." → "Our engine analyzes your sales data and flags unusual drops."

### 5. Explain each term where it first appears

Follow the page in reading order and give a one-line explanation wherever a term first shows up — even if a later section covers it in depth.

- Reading order includes elements seen before section headings: intro animations, hero blocks, scroll scenes, cards.
- Don't let an earlier sentence lean on a concept introduced later. If it must, add a short explanation in place.

### 6. Keep teaser lines next to a definition

Hero lines, teasers, and one-line scroll captions may put impression first. But a definition must appear on the same screen or the very next one. Never chain teaser lines with no definition in sight.

- OK: "One button. Done." with "A note app that records and summarizes your meetings." right below it.
- OK: "2 million users." right after the section that says what the app does.

### 7. The user's material is the source

- If the material contains a definition, use its wording.
- If it doesn't, build the definition only from facts in the material. Tell the user which definitions you assembled this way when you hand over the copy.
- Do not invent facts, numbers, relationships, or reasons that aren't in the material — not even plausible inferences.
- Keep the level of certainty. If the material says "is said to", "claims", or "undecided", keep it that way.
- If pieces of the material contradict each other, don't pick one; tell the user.
- Content marked private, spoiler, author-only, or internal stays off public pages, including phrasing that hints at it.

### 8. Don't overcorrect

- A definition takes one or two sentences. Don't turn every heading into a dictionary entry.
- Keep the page's voice and mood.
- Don't repeat the same definition everywhere. Give it once where the concept first appears; afterwards, refer to it briefly.

### 9. Write in the page's language

These principles apply in any language. Write the copy in the language of the page or the user's material, not the language of this skill.

## Workflow

1. Read all of the material. If you read only part of it, tell the user which part.
2. List the concepts the page will cover and write a one-line definition for each. Mark whether it comes from the material or was assembled from it.
3. Fix the reading order of the page and find where each concept first appears.
4. At each of those places, write the definition first, then the role, details, and limits.
5. Run the check below before handing over.
6. When handing over, briefly list assembled definitions, gaps left empty because the material didn't cover them, and contradictions found in the material.

## Check before handing over

- [ ] Reading only the first sentence of each section and card, a first-time reader can tell what each concept is.
- [ ] No heading where a concept first appears consists only of a negation, number, metaphor, or oddity.
- [ ] Verbs like "analyzes" or "protects" have objects.
- [ ] Every term is explained where it first appears in reading order.
- [ ] Every teaser line has a definition on the same or the next screen.
- [ ] No facts were invented, and certainty markers like "is said to" or "claims" are unchanged.
- [ ] No private or spoiler content, and nothing that hints at it.

## Examples

### Feature card

Bad:

> **No more waiting.**
> Everything is processed instantly.

Good:

> **Auto-pay**
> A feature that charges your saved card on the same day every month. Payments go through without waiting for approval.

### Section subtitle

Bad:

> **Partner Studios** — Not an official program.

Good:

> **Partner Studios** — Independent studios we work with on client projects. It's an informal network, not an official program.

### Term on first appearance

Bad:

> Earn **tokens** with every lesson. Tokens never expire.

Good:

> Earn **tokens** — the in-app currency you spend on hints — with every lesson. Tokens never expire.

### Character profile

Bad:

> No one knows her real name.

Good:

> A smuggler who runs the night ferry between the two port cities. No one knows her real name.

### Fictional place

Bad:

> **The Lantern Market**
> Opens only at low tide. No one has ever counted its stalls.

Good:

> **The Lantern Market**
> A floating bazaar in the harbor where traders sell goods banned on land. It opens only at low tide, and no one has ever counted its stalls.
